Fair Scales, Honest Models

Why do models care about units, and how do they avoid simply memorising?

Is a gap of 0.10 m as big as a gap of 20 kg?→scaling: put features on a fair footing Will it still work on essays it has never seen?→generalization: and regularization to get there first the units, then the memorising

You will watch k-means fail on the wrong units and succeed on fair ones, then watch a curve memorise some essays and two kinds of penalty, L2 and L1, calm it down.

1 · Two measurements

{{n}} made-up people

Every dot is one person we invented: height in metres across, body weight in kilograms up. Nothing here is real data. We want a computer to find the groups hiding among these dots, without being told who belongs where.

1 · Two measurements

Two hidden groups

We secretly built the dots from two groups, A (circles) and B (diamonds). They differ mainly in height: no A person is taller than {{aMaxH}} m, no B person shorter than {{bMinH}} m. Their weights overlap almost completely.

A good method should rediscover exactly this split.

2 · What “close” means

Distance adds up squared gaps

A computer calls two people close when the distance between their numbers is small. That distance squares each gap and adds them up.

\(d=\sqrt{(\Delta\text{height})^2+(\Delta\text{weight})^2}\)

Take 1.75 m, 85 kg and 1.65 m, 65 kg. The gaps are 0.10 and 20, squared 0.01 and 400: weight supplies {{exShare}} of the squared distance. Click two dots, or use the buttons.

2 · What “close” means

What k-means sees

Draw both axes to the same scale, one unit across for one unit up, and height shrinks to a sliver: 0.1 m is nothing next to 20 kg. Even a tall and a short person of similar weight, {{xgDh}} m apart, are at distance only {{xgD}}, far closer than the 20.0 above.

3 · k-means in 60 seconds

Assign, move, repeat

k-means looks for k groups, here two. It starts with a centre on each of two people, then repeats two moves: give every person to the nearest centre; move each centre to the average of its people. It stops when nothing changes. Scroll, or use the buttons under the figure.

3 · k-means in 60 seconds

Run it on metres and kilograms

It stops after {{rawIter}} rounds. Blue and orange are the clusters it found; circle or diamond is the true group. They are mixed up: only {{rawAgree}} of the people match, barely better than a coin. The dividing line is nearly flat, so k-means split people by weight, not by height.

3 · k-means in 60 seconds

Change the unit, change the answer

Measure height in centimetres instead of metres: same 40 people, but the height gap grows from 0.10 to 10. The clusters change completely: {{cmAgree}} now match. A result that depends on the unit is not a finding about people. (Weight in grams would break it again: {{gAgree}}.)

4 · Scaling fixes it

Normalization: squeeze into 0 to 1

Feature scaling puts features on comparable ranges. Min–max normalization maps each feature's smallest value to 0 and its largest to 1.

\(x'=\dfrac{x-\min}{\max-\min}\)

Beware: “normalization” also names other recipes, such as scaling a vector to length 1 (as for cosine similarity). Sources disagree; this page means min–max.

4 · Scaling fixes it

Standardization: how many typical spreads from average

Standardization (the z-score) subtracts the average and divides by the standard deviation, the typical spread. A z of +1 means one typical spread above average.

\(z=\dfrac{x-\text{mean}}{\text{std}}\)

Either way both features now matter equally, and k-means recovers {{zAgree}} of the true groups.

4 · Scaling fixes it

Same people, three versions

Raw units give wrong groups; min–max and z-score give {{mmAgree}} and {{zAgree}}. The star is a new person arriving later: scaled with the minimum, maximum, mean and spread of the original 40, never recomputed from new data, then given to the nearest centre.

4 · Scaling fixes it

One extreme value

A typing slip puts one person at {{outW}} kg. Min–max divides by the range, which that value now controls: everybody else keeps only {{mmKeep}} of their spread. The standard deviation grows too, but less: z-scores keep {{zKeep}}. Neither is immune; robust scalers based on the median exist.

5 · Generalization

A curve through {{nTrain}} essays

Blue dots are {{nTrain}} made-up essays: average sentence length against a readability rating. We fit a polynomial curve; its degree sets how bendy it may be (1 is a straight line, 12 can wiggle eleven times). Drag the slider: the training error, the average squared miss on these essays, keeps falling.

5 · Generalization

Then fresh essays arrive

What matters is generalization: doing well on new data, not just the data the model learned from. The orange diamonds are {{nTest}} fresh essays the curve has never seen.

Predict: will the degree-12 curve beat the straight line on the fresh essays?

No. Degree 12 misses by {{test12}} on average, the line by {{test1}}. The curve memorised the noise in the training essays: overfitting.
5 · Generalization

Too simple, too bendy

Training error only ever falls. Test error, measured on essays the model never saw, falls and then climbs: degree {{bestDeg}} is the sweet spot (test error {{testBest}}). Too simple is underfitting; too flexible is overfitting. Slide to see where you are on the two curves.

6 · L2 (ridge)

Make big coefficients expensive

Generalization is the goal; regularization is one tool for it. L2 (ridge) adds a penalty to the loss: the squared size of every coefficient, times a price λ. Slide λ up: the curve calms down, the bars shrink, but none ever reaches exactly 0.

\(\text{loss}=\text{average squared miss}+\lambda\sum_j w_j^2\)

The w are the model's coefficients, its weights. Degree stays at 12.

6 · L2 (ridge)

Not too little, not too much

With λ = 0 the curve memorises: {{ridgeT0}} on fresh essays. A modest λ brings that down to {{ridgeBest}}. A huge λ flattens everything into underfitting: {{ridgeEnd}}. In practice you pick λ on separate validation data, not on the final test essays.

7 · L1 (lasso)

A penalty with corners

L1 (lasso) penalizes the sum of the coefficients' absolute sizes instead. Slide λ up: coefficients do not just shrink, they land on exactly 0 and drop out. At the best λ only {{lassoKept}} of the 12 terms remain. Choosing features automatically like this is called feature selection.

\(\text{loss}=\text{average squared miss}+\lambda\sum_j |w_j|\)
7 · L1 (lasso)

L1 against L2, side by side

Each line follows one coefficient as λ grows. Under L2 they all slide gently toward 0 and never arrive. Under L1 they hit 0 one by one and stay there: at its best λ, L1 has {{lassoZeros}} exact zeros, L2 has {{ridgeZeros}}.

7 · L1 (lasso)

Why corners give zeros optional

Picture just two coefficients. The grey rings join equal loss around the unpenalized best answer; the penalty confines the answer to a region: a circle for L2, a diamond for L1. The first ring to touch the diamond usually touches a corner, where one coefficient is exactly 0. A circle has no corners.

8 · Scaling and penalties

Same information, different units

A penalty adds up coefficients, so it cares about units. Take {{nU}} made-up essays and the share of rare words in each, written as a fraction (0 to 0.3) or as a percent (0 to 30): the same facts. As a fraction its coefficient is 100 times bigger, so ridge crushes it: {{fracKept}} survives, against {{pctKept}} as a percent.

8 · Scaling and penalties

The fix: standardize first

Standardized, every feature is on the same footing and the penalty treats them alike, in whatever units: {{stdKept}} of both survives. That is why our degree-12 curves used standardized columns: x¹² is astronomically bigger than x. The scaling numbers come from the training essays only.

9 · Recap

Three words for resizing features

Scaling is the umbrella; normalization (min–max) and standardization (z-score) are two recipes. Learn the numbers on the training data, then apply them to everything else.

9 · Recap

A goal and its tools

Generalization is the goal: working on new data. Regularization is one tool for it, in two flavours. L2 shrinks everything, L1 switches some terms off.

9 · Recap

Where you will meet this

In language technology these ideas hide inside document vectors, clustering, and every text classifier you train.
