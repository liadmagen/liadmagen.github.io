Pointwise Mutual Information

A scroll-driven visual tour of PMI: how counting word co-occurrences in a tiny corpus, comparing them against a chance baseline, and taking a logarithm gives a principled measure of association between words — the workhorse behind collocation extraction, PPMI word matrices and modern word embeddings. Inspired by R2D3.

1 · Collocations

Some word pairs just belong together

“strong tea” is a natural English phrase; “powerful tea” is grammatical but odd. Both are just two words near each other — so what actually separates them? How often they co-occur. We count word pairs in a tiny toy English corpus (21 hard-coded sentences), computed live in your browser by pmi-math.js.

1 · Collocations

Count co-occurrences in a window

Each word is paired with every vocabulary word within a window of ±3 positions in the same sentence. Around tea the spokes thicken with the count: strong × 7, coffee × 3, morning × 3, …

1 · Collocations

“powerful × tea” is rare — but is rarity the signal?

It happens only once in the corpus. But “powerful” also appears next to engine (×3) and computer (×3) — so a small count alone does not make a collocation, and a big count alone does not either.

1 · Collocations

Raw counts mislead: frequent words co-occur with everything

“strong” is a frequent word, so it co-occurs with lots of things by chance. The dashed spokes show the count we would expect under independence. To judge a pair we must compare observed vs. expected — that baseline is the next chapter.

2 · Counts → probabilities

The co-occurrence matrix F

All pair counts live in one matrix: rows and columns are the 10 vocabulary words, cell fij = how often word i and word j co-occur in the window. Darker cell = bigger count. The matrix is symmetric — the pair (tea, strong) counts once.

2 · Counts → probabilities

Marginals: row and column sums

Summing a row gives fi* = Σj fij — how many pairs involve word i (SLP3 J.5). The edge bars show these marginals: tea is the most connected word (16), storm the least (1).

2 · Counts → probabilities

Joint probabilities: pij = fij / N

Divide every cell by the total N = sum of all cells (= 68 here) and the counts become a proper joint distribution: \(p_{ij} = f_{ij}/N, \quad \sum_{ij} p_{ij} = 1\) Cells now show per-mille values.

2 · Counts → probabilities

From counts to a probability distribution

N counts every (ordered) pair in the window — 68 for our corpus. \(p(x,y) = \tfrac{f(x,y)}{N}, \qquad p(x) = \textstyle\sum_y p(x,y)\) With p(x,y), p(x) and p(y) we have everything PMI needs.

3 · The independence baseline

Observed counts in a 2×2 contingency table

For the pair (tea, strong) the corpus gives a 2×2 table: a = 7 (both), b = 4 (strong only), c = 9 (tea only), d = 48 (neither). The cells sum to N = 68.

3 · The independence baseline

Expected under independence: E = fi·fj / N

If the words were independent, we would expect \(E[f_{ij}] = N\,p(x)p(y) = \tfrac{f_i f_j}{N} = \tfrac{11 \cdot 16}{68} \approx 2.59\) co-occurrences of tea×strong. We observed 7 — more than chance. The bars compare them.

3 · The independence baseline

“powerful × tea”: observed 1 < expected 1.88

Flip the pair and the bars flip: powerful × tea is observed once but expected ~1.88 times under independence — the words repel each other (ratio < 1).

3 · The independence baseline

The ratio obs/exp decides everything

Every pair gets one number: ratio = observed / expected. \(\text{ratio} = \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} = \tfrac{\text{observed}}{\text{expected}}\) ratio > 1 → association, = 1 → independence, < 1 → repulsion. PMI is simply the logarithm of this ratio.

4 · The PMI formula

PMI = log₂ of the obs/exp ratio

Pointwise mutual information (Church & Hanks 1989; Fano 1961) is defined as \(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}\) In counts (SLP3 J.2/J.3): \(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log_2 \frac{f(x,y)\, N}{f(x)\, f(y)}\)

4 · The PMI formula

Massage it: p(x,y) = p(x|y)·p(y)

Substitute the chain rule and one p(y) cancels: \(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log_2 \frac{p(x\,|\,y)}{p(x)}\) Read it as: “how much more likely is x, given that y happened, than x alone?” That conditional-probability reading builds all the intuition.

4 · The PMI formula

…and symmetrically

Using p(x,y) = p(y|x)·p(x) instead: \(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log_2 \frac{p(y\,|\,x)}{p(y)}\) Same number, mirrored question: “how much does seeing x raise the odds of y?” PMI(x,y) = PMI(y,x).

4 · The PMI formula

Die example: P(5 | result > 4) = 1/2, but P(5) = 1/6

Roll a die. Seeing a 5 is rare (1/6), but given the result is > 4, a 5 happens half the time. So \(\mathrm{PMI} = \log_2 \tfrac{1/2}{1/6} = \log_2 3 \approx \mathbf{1.58}\ \text{bits}\) The needle on the gauge swings to +1.58 — a strong association between “5” and “> 4”.

4 · The PMI formula

A gauge for association — in bits

Because we use log₂, PMI is measured in bits: each doubling of the ratio adds 1 bit. The needle shows PMI(tea, strong) ≈ +1.44 (association) and PMI(tea, powerful) ≈ −0.91 (repulsion), with 0 = independence in the middle.

5 · Reading a PMI value

PMI bars for every observed pair

Each bar is one pair’s PMI in bits (computed live from the corpus). Right of the zero line = association, left = repulsion. Note how the top bar belongs to a pair that occurred only once…

5 · Reading a PMI value

> 0 association · = 0 independence · < 0 repulsion

PMI > 0: the pair occurs more than chance predicts. PMI = 0: exactly independence (ratio 1). PMI < 0: the pair avoids each other. Since log₂ is used, the unit is bits: PMI = 1 means “twice as likely as chance”.

5 · Reading a PMI value

Caveat: rare events inflate PMI — and log(0) = −∞

powerful × storm happened once, yet tops the chart at +3.09 bits: with tiny counts, the 1/p(x) factor in log(p(x|y)/p(x)) weighs heavily. And storm × tea never occurred: log(0) = −∞ (hatched bar). SLP3 warns: negative PMI on rare events is unreliable.

5 · Reading a PMI value

PPMI = max(PMI, 0)

The standard fix (SLP3 J.4): clip negatives to zero — the positive PMI. \(\mathrm{PPMI}(x,y) = \max\big(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y),\ 0\big)\) Watch the red bars flatten onto the zero line.

6 · Worked example

The full PMI matrix, computed live

Every cell of the 10×10 matrix colored by its PMI value — red = repulsion, neutral = 0, blue = association, all in bits. Zero-count cells are −∞ and drawn hatched.

6 · Worked example

The strongest cells

Labeled: powerful × storm +3.09 (a once-seen pair — inflated), powerful × computer +2.67, powerful × engine +2.35, and our hero tea × strong +1.44 — while tea × powerful −0.91 sits on the red side.

6 · Worked example

The PPMI matrix: negatives clipped

Apply PPMI = max(PMI, 0) to every cell: the red cells fade to neutral. What remains is a sparse, non-negative association matrix — exactly what NLP systems feed into SVD or use as word-context features.

6 · Worked example

Read it off

The chip shows the live numbers: PMI(tea, strong) = +1.44 bits, PPMI(tea, powerful) = 0, PMI(storm, tea) = −∞ → PPMI 0. A term–term matrix like this is the classic alternative to tf-idf for finding collocations (Church & Hanks 1989).

7 · PMI vs MI

Every observed pair is an event

Each bubble is one pair (x, y) that occurred: area ∝ p(x,y) (its joint probability) and color = sign of PMI (blue = association, red = repulsion). Rare pairs are small bubbles.

7 · PMI vs MI

MI = E[PMI] = Σ p(x,y)·PMI(x,y)

Mutual information (SLP3 J.1) is just the expectation of PMI under the joint distribution: \(I(X,Y) = \textstyle\sum_x \sum_y p(x,y)\ \log_2 \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{PMI}]\) Scroll and the bubbles are absorbed one by one into the running sum…

7 · PMI vs MI

Watch the running weighted average converge

The right-hand readout accumulates p(x,y)·PMI(x,y) bubble by bubble. Positive and negative contributions mix, and the sum settles at the MI of the whole joint distribution.

7 · PMI vs MI

MI ≈ 1.31 bits for our toy corpus

PMI is a per-event score (one pair, can be negative); MI is the average of PMI over all events — and an average of log-ratios against the independence baseline is always ≥ 0. MI answers “how many bits, on average, does seeing one word tell me about the other?”

From corpus to PPMI matrix

The whole pipeline in three panels: toy corpus → windowed count matrix F → normalized PPMI matrix. Everything on this page is exactly this pipeline, computed in your browser.

8 · PMI in NLP practice

Build it in Python with Counter + numpy

The same pipeline in a few lines — the standard way to build a term–term matrix: 

from collections import Counter
import numpy as np

WINDOW = 3
tokens = [w for sent in CORPUS for w in sent.lower().split()]
vocab  = sorted({w for w in tokens if w in VOCAB})
F = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)))
for pos, w in enumerate(tokens):
    for q in range(pos - WINDOW, pos + WINDOW + 1):  # symmetric window
        if q != pos and 0 <= q < len(tokens) and tokens[q] in vocab:
            F[vocab.index(w), vocab.index(tokens[q])] += 1

N  = F.sum()                    # total co-occurrence pairs
P  = F / N                      # joint probabilities p(x, y)
px = P.sum(axis=1)             # marginals p(x)
PMI  = np.where(F > 0, np.log2(P / np.outer(px, px)), -np.inf)
PPMI = np.maximum(PMI, 0)       # clip negatives (SLP3 J.4)

8 · PMI in NLP practice

Word embeddings implicitly factorize (P)PMI

Levy & Goldberg (2014) showed that SGNS / word2vec’s skip-gram objective is approximately an implicit factorization of the shifted PMI matrix — so the dots on the right are what “strong” and “tea” become: near each other, far from “storm”. Variants of PMI used in practice: PPMI, conditional PMI (C-PMI), and significance-adjusted PMI (Hoeffding bounds).

9 · Recap

The three formulas

\(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \log_2 \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} = \log_2 \tfrac{p(x|y)}{p(x)}\) \(\mathrm{PPMI}(x,y) = \max(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y), 0)\) \(I(X,Y) = \textstyle\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\,\mathrm{PMI}(x,y) = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{PMI}] \ge 0\)

9 · Recap

Caveats

Rare words inflate PMI (small p(x) makes 1/p(x) big) — and unseen pairs give log(0) = −∞, so negative PMI on rare events is unreliable: use PPMI. Estimates get more reliable with bigger corpora. PMI is the classic alternative to tf-idf for collocations (Church & Hanks 1989) and the implicit target of word2vec-style embeddings.

9 · Recap

When to use PMI

Use it to score word–word or word–context association: collocation extraction, term–term / term–document matrices, feature weighting in classification, and as the theoretical lens on embeddings. Watch the sign, the units (bits), and the rarity of the events.
