Introduction to ML for Language Technologies

Which words belong together?

Teaching a computer what translators already know.

EnglishGerman tea strong tea
powerful tea starker Tee rain heavy rain starker Regen decision make a decision
do a decision eine Entscheidung treffen
eine Entscheidung machen

Nobody taught you these pairings as rules — you absorbed them from reading. Can a machine discover them just by counting?

You will count word pairs, compare the counts with what chance would give, and take a logarithm. The result, pointwise mutual information (PMI), is the “MI score” that corpus tools such as Sketch Engine show next to collocates, and it is the idea behind bilingual-lexicon induction and word embeddings.

2 · Counting isn't enough

Who keeps company with tea?

Each spoke is a pair; thicker means counted more often. strong leads with {{count tea strong}}.

But coffee was paired with tea {{count tea coffee}} times, as often as morning. Is “tea coffee” a collocation?

Probably not. Tea and coffee are both common words, and they often stand in the same slot (“tea or coffee”). A high count can simply mean that both words are frequent.
2 · Counting isn't enough

Frequent words meet everyone

Circle size now shows count(x): in how many pairs a word takes part. tea is the most frequent word ({{cnt tea}}); strong has {{cnt strong}}.

Big words bump into each other more often by chance. So for every pair we should ask: how many pairs would chance alone give?

3 · What would chance give?

Every pair has two ends

{{pairs}} pairs, two ends each: {{N}} ends in total. We call that number N. tea fills {{cnt tea}} of them ({{share tea}}), strong fills {{cnt strong}} ({{share strong}}).

3 · What would chance give?

Shuffle the ends

What if words paired up purely by chance? Put all {{N}} ends in a bag, shuffle, and deal them out in pairs. Count the tea + strong pairs. Repeat. (Press the button to deal once more.)

3 · What would chance give?

Chance hovers around {{exp tea strong}}

We get this without shuffling: multiply the two shares and the total. \(\text{expected}(x,y)=\dfrac{\text{count}(x)\cdot\text{count}(y)}{N}=\dfrac{ {{t:cnt tea}}\cdot {{t:cnt strong}} }{ {{t:N}} }\approx {{t:exp tea strong}}\) We actually saw {{count tea strong}}. In the shuffles, {{shufhit}} of the {{shuftrials}} deals reached that.

3 · What would chance give?

More or less than chance?

Seen more than expected: the words attract each other. Seen less: they avoid each other. The ratio seen ÷ expected tells us how strongly.

4 · From ratio to bits

Ratios are lopsided

Twice as often as chance gives ratio 2. Half as often gives ratio ½. Equally strong, opposite directions — yet on a normal number line they sit unevenly around 1. A logarithm repairs that.

4 · From ratio to bits

Logarithms in 60 seconds

The base-2 logarithm asks: how many times must I double? Ratio 2 → 1 bit. Ratio 1 → 0 bits. Ratio ½ → −1 bit. Ratio 4 → 2 bits (doubled twice). Drag the handle or press a ratio button.

4 · From ratio to bits

PMI = bits of “seen ÷ expected”

tea + strong: ratio {{ratio tea strong}}, so {{pmi tea strong}} bits. tea + powerful: ratio {{ratio tea powerful}}, so {{pmi tea powerful}} bits. Positive: attract. Zero: chance. Negative: avoid.

\(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y)=\log_2\dfrac{\text{count}(x,y)}{\text{expected}(x,y)}=\log_2\dfrac{\text{count}(x,y)\cdot N}{\text{count}(x)\cdot\text{count}(y)}\)
The same thing in textbook notation

Divide every count by N to get probabilities, \(P(x,y)=\dfrac{\text{count}(x,y)}{N},\quad P(x)=\dfrac{\text{count}(x)}{N}\) and PMI becomes \(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y)=\log_2\dfrac{P(x,y)}{P(x)\,P(y)}\)

4 · From ratio to bits

Another way to read it

A fair die shows a 5 with chance 1 in 6. If you are told the result is above 4, the chance of a 5 is 1 in 2: {{dieratio}} times likelier. That is \(\log_2 \dfrac{1/2}{1/6} = \log_2 {{t:dieratio}} \approx {{t:diepmi}}\ \text{bits}\) PMI asks: how much does seeing one word raise the chance of the other?

6 · When PMI misleads

Rare pairs shoot to the top

The highest PMI belongs to powerful storm: seen once, while chance expects only {{exp powerful storm}}. One sighting looks like a huge surprise.

Which pair has the higher PMI: tea strong (seen {{count tea strong}}×) or powerful storm (seen {{count powerful storm}}×)?

powerful storm: {{pmi powerful storm}} against {{pmi tea strong}}. Rare words with a single sighting are unreliable evidence.
6 · When PMI misleads

Fix 1: a minimum count

Corpus tools let you set a minimum frequency. Tick the box: the one-off pairs drop out and {{nmin2}} pairs remain, with a far more sensible top.

6 · When PMI misleads

Never seen: minus infinity

A pair that never occurred has count 0, and the logarithm of 0 is −∞. Negative PMI from tiny counts is unreliable anyway, so many systems keep only the positive part: \(\mathrm{PPMI}(x,y)=\max\big(\mathrm{PMI}(x,y),\,0\big)\)

6 · When PMI misleads

Fix 2: smoothing

Another trick: estimate the second word's frequency from \(\text{count}^{0.75}\). Rare words get a little extra probability, so their PMI shrinks (powerful storm: {{pmi powerful storm}} → {{smooth powerful storm}}); frequent words rise slightly. ◆ marks the smoothed value.

8 · Going further

MI: the average PMI optional

PMI scores one pair. Weight each pair's PMI by how often it happens and add up: you get mutual information, here {{mi}} bits, a score for the corpus as a whole. \(\mathrm{MI}=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\,\mathrm{PMI}(x,y)\)

8 · Going further

From PMI to a map of words optional

Keep the positive PMI cells (PPMI) and each word gets a row of numbers. A matrix technique (SVD) squeezes each row to two numbers. This is the real result for our ten words. The two biggest directions both describe the powerful group, so we plot the biggest direction of each group.

8 · Going further

Word embeddings optional

Levy & Goldberg (2014) showed that word2vec (skip-gram with negative sampling) implicitly factorizes a word–context matrix of PMI shifted by \(\log k\), where k is the number of negative samples.

8 · Going further

Do it yourself in Python optional

This reproduces every number on this page: windows stay inside sentences, and log of zero is masked.

import numpy as np

WINDOW = 3
vocab = ["tea", "coffee", "strong", "powerful", "hot",
         "bitter", "morning", "engine", "storm", "computer"]
idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
corpus = ["strong tea wakes me up", ...]  # the sentences above

F = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)))
for sentence in corpus:
    tokens = sentence.split()
    for i, x in enumerate(tokens):
        for j in range(i + 1, min(i + WINDOW + 1, len(tokens))):
            y = tokens[j]            # same sentence only
            if x in idx and y in idx:
                F[idx[x], idx[y]] += 1
                F[idx[y], idx[x]] += 1

N = F.sum()                          # two ends per pair
count = F.sum(axis=1)
expected = np.outer(count, count) / N
with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
    PMI = np.log2(F / expected)      # -inf where count is 0
PPMI = np.maximum(PMI, 0)
9 · Recap

Three moves

Count how often two words are paired. Work out what chance would give. Take the log₂ of seen ÷ expected. Positive means attraction, zero chance, negative avoidance.

9 · Recap

What this means for you

When a corpus tool shows an “MI score” beside a collocate, this is what it computed. A high score with a decent frequency marks a candidate worth checking for your glossary. A high score on a handful of occurrences deserves suspicion (chapter 6).
