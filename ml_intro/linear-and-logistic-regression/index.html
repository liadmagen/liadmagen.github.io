Introduction to ML for Language Technologies

From a Number to a Yes

How does a computer learn to give a score, and then to answer yes or no?

How readable is this essay?→a number: 62 out of 100 Is it readable for a first-year?→a decision: yes the first is linear regression, the second logistic regression

Both use the same machine: a straight line, plus one small bend. You will draw the line yourself, see how wrong it is, and watch a computer slide downhill to the best one.

The data on this page is made up: {{n}} invented essays and {{snN}} invented emails, so that we can see every dot at once. The ideas are the ones behind spam filters and many text classifiers.

1 · Data and a line

{{n}} made-up essays, one hunch

Every dot is one essay. Across: its average sentence length, in words. Up: a teacher's readability rating, 0 to 100. (We invented these essays, so that every dot is visible.)

Notice the drift: longer sentences, lower ratings. Machine learning starts with a hunch like this.

1 · Data and a line

Draw your best line

Predict first: where would you draw a line that turns sentence length into a rating? Say it out loud, then try.

Grab the two handles (or use the sliders). A line is two numbers: where it starts and how steeply it falls. The caption scores your line with one number, the loss. Lower is better.

2 · How wrong is a line?

Gaps become squares

The line is a machine: sentence length in, a rating out. Drop a segment from each essay to the line: that gap is the residual. Now turn each gap into a real square.

Gaps above and below the line would cancel out; squares do not. And a big miss costs far more than a small one.

2 · How wrong is a line?

Every line has one number

Average the {{n}} squares: the mean squared error, in words the average squared miss. Bad lines score big, better lines small. The best line here scores about {{mse}}.

\(\mathrm{MSE}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i-\hat y_i\big)^2\)

y is the real rating, ŷ (“y-hat”) the line's answer. Linear regression has one obsession: the smallest MSE.

3 · Finding the best line

The hot–cold game

A model cannot eyeball the dots as you did. It plays hot and cold: stand on a line, feel which way the loss falls fastest, take a small step, repeat. This loop is gradient descent.

On the right every point is one possible line, coloured by its loss (red hot, yellow cold). The ball is the model.

3 · Finding the best line

Why a long, thin valley?

The rings join lines with equal loss. They are long and thin because start and slope trade off: a steeper line needs a higher start to stay in the middle of the dots.

Slide along the valley floor and the line just pivots around the middle, losing little. (We rescaled sentence length first, as real tools do, to make the descent smooth.)

3 · Finding the best line

How big is a step? learning rate

The learning rate is the size of each step. Too small and training crawls: after {{smallSteps}} steps the loss is still {{lossSmall}}. Too big and it overshoots the valley and the loss explodes. Just right settles in {{goodSteps}} steps. Press the buttons to compare.

3 · Finding the best line

The finished machine

The descent ends at \(\hat y = {{t:b0}} - {{t:b1}}\,x\) In words: each extra word per sentence costs about {{b1r}} rating points. The {{b0}} is the rating of a 0-word sentence; no essay has one, so do not read meaning into it.

A new essay with 12-word sentences gets ŷ ≈ {{pred12}}. Features in, a number out.

4 · A different question

A yes/no question

Now the writing program wants a decision, not a rating: is this essay readable for a first-year? We say yes when the teacher's rating is at least {{pass}}: {{nRead}} essays are readable, {{nNot}} are not.

Watch the dots collapse into two rows. Each keeps its sentence length.

4 · A different question

The number machine fails

We already own a machine that outputs numbers. Why not fit the line to the 1s and 0s?

Predict: what will this line answer for a very short sentence, 6 words?

More than 1: {{ll6}}. At 28 words it says {{ll28}}, a negative probability. The line answers a question nobody asked, in units that do not exist.
5 · Squish, then decide

Squish the line the sigmoid

For yes/no the line is learned again: its score z is evidence. Big positive z says readable, big negative says not. Before anyone sees it, push z through the S-curve σ: any number lands between 0 and 1, a probability.

\(z=\beta_0+\beta_1x,\qquad \sigma(z)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\)

Here z = {{lb0}} − {{lb1}}·x. Linear regression, wrapped in a bend.

5 · Squish, then decide

From probability to decision

Pick a threshold, 0.5 to start: above it, say yes. Where the curve crosses it is the decision boundary, near {{bound}}-word sentences. The model gets {{nCorrect}} of {{n}} right; the {{nMiss}} circled essays are its mistakes.

5 · Squish, then decide

How it learns: log-loss optional

Squared error suits numbers. For probabilities the penalty is minus the log of the probability given to the right answer: small if the model was right and sure, huge if it was sure and wrong.

\(\text{penalty}=-\ln p_{\text{right}}\)

Averaged over the {{n}} essays: log-loss {{logloss}}.

5 · Squish, then decide

What the slope means: odds optional

Odds are probability over its complement: 75% is 3 to 1. Each extra word multiplies the odds of “readable” by \(e^{\beta_1}=e^{ {{t:lb1neg}} }\approx {{t:odds}}\) a cut of about {{oddsCut}}, wherever you start.

6 · Spam and recap

Same machine, new problem: spam

This is your spam filter, in miniature: {{snN}} made-up emails and one feature, how often the word free appears. The curve says {{snMin}} times is more likely spam than not. Slide the threshold.

Predict: if you raise the threshold, do false alarms go up or down?

Down: {{spamFa 0.5}} real emails flagged at 0.5, {{spamFa 0.8}} at 0.8. The price: more spam slips through. Every deployed classifier is this slider.
6 · Spam and recap

Same machine, two lenses

Linear regression answers a number; logistic regression wraps the same weighted sum in σ and answers a probability. Real models add more measurements: \(\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2+\cdots\) Swap σ for softmax for many classes; stack such machines and you are walking toward neural networks.
