From a Number to a Yes

Act 1 · the number machine

Forty essays, one hunch

Every dot is an essay written by a first-year student. Along the bottom: the average length of its sentences, in words. Up the side: a readability score from 0 to 100.

Notice the drift — longer sentences, lower scores. Machine learning starts exactly here: a hunch that one measurement might predict the other.

Act 1 · scene 2 · your turn

Draw your best line

Predict first: before you touch anything — where would you draw a line that turns sentence length into a score? Say your answer out loud, then try it.

Grab the two handles and move them. The chip keeps score: the total squared distance between your line and the forty essays. In class we call this number the loss. Lower is better.

Act 1 · scene 3

How wrong is a line?

A line is a machine: sentence length goes in, a score comes out. To measure how wrong it is, drop a line from each essay’s true score to the line — that gap is the residual.

Now the strange-but-brilliant part: turn each gap into an actual square. Why? Gaps above and below the line cancel each other out — squares don’t. And big misses get punished much more than small ones.

Act 1 · scene 4

Every line has a number

Add up all the squares and you get one number: the total squared error. Every possible line has one — terrible lines have huge numbers, better lines smaller ones.

Linear regression has a single obsession: find the line with the smallest number. Keep your own score in mind — the model will try to beat it.

Act 1 · scene 5

The hot–cold game (gradient descent)

A model can’t eyeball forty dots the way you just did. It plays the children’s hot–cold game: stand on a line, feel which way is downhill — the slope of the loss — take a small step, repeat.

In the right-hand map, height is error. The model is the ball: blind to the essays, rolling into the valley. This loop is gradient descent — and it is how almost everything in this course learns.

Act 1 · scene 6

How big is a step? (learning rate)

One knob rules the whole game: the learning rate — the size of each step. Too small, and training crawls; you’ll retire before it arrives.

Too large, and the model overshoots the valley and the loss explodes. Just right, and it settles calmly at the bottom. Every real training run you will ever debug is this exact story.

Act 1 · scene 7

The machine, assembled

The finished model is embarrassingly small: ŷ = 93.3 − 2.19 · x. Two numbers — intercept and slope — are everything it learned.

Feed it a new essay with 12-word sentences and it answers a number. Not a guess — a prediction. Linear regression: features in, a number out.

Act 2 · the pivot

A different question

Now the writing program changes its mind. It doesn’t want a score from 0 to 100 — it wants a decision: is this essay readable for a first-year? Yes or no?

Watch the same forty essays collapse into two rows. Every essay keeps its sentence-length position. Everything else melts away.

Act 2 · scene 9

The number machine fails

We already own a machine that outputs numbers — why not point it at the 1s and 0s?

Fitted to the labels, the line dutifully answers 1.07 for short sentences — “7% more than fully readable”? — and −0.23 for long ones. Negative readability. The old machine is answering a question nobody asked, in units that don’t exist.

Act 2 · scene 10

Squish the line (the sigmoid)

The fix is famous. Keep the line — its score z = β0 + β1x still measures evidence — but before showing it to anyone, push it through a squishing function:

σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

The S-curve takes any number, however wild, and squashes it into 0…1 — where it can finally be read as a probability. That is the whole trick: linear regression, wrapped in a non-linear function.

Act 2 · scene 11

From probability to decision

Every essay now gets a probability of being readable. To decide, pick a threshold — 0.5 by default. Where the curve crosses it, the model flips from “not readable” to “readable”: that x is the decision boundary, at ≈ 15-word sentences.

The essays it gets wrong are circled — 33 of 40 correct here. Behind the scenes, the curve was trained by log-loss, the logistic cousin of squared error: being confident and wrong hurts a lot.

Act 2 · scene 12 · your turn

Same machine, new problem: spam

This is not a readability trick — it’s your spam filter. In the notebook you’ll train this exact machine on emails, with one feature: how many times the word free appears. The fitted curve already says “free” three times is more-likely-than-not spam.

Modify: slide the threshold. Cautious (0.8) — few real emails get flagged, but spam slips through. Aggressive (0.5) — more spam caught, at a price. What would your inbox prefer?

Every deployed classifier is this slider.

Act 3 · coda

Same machine, two lenses

Linear regression predicts a number. Logistic regression predicts a probability. And it is literally the same machine — a weighted sum of features, wrapped in a non-linear function.

Sigmoid for two classes; swap in softmax and you have multiclass; stack these machines and you’re walking toward neural networks. Next stop: the notebook — where logistic regression faces your Naive Bayes spam filter head-to-head.

Linear regression

Features in, a number out: ŷ = β0 + β1x. Trained by making the squared error small — by hand, you drew it; the model uses gradient descent.

Logistic regression

The same weighted sum, wrapped in σ (or softmax for many classes): a probability, a threshold, a decision. Fast to train — and the last layer of many a neural network.
