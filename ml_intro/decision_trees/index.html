Teaching a computer to ask the right questions

Is this text English or German? You see it at a glance. A computer needs something more mechanical: a short list of yes/no questions about things it can measure.

Does the text contain the letter ß?
yesGerman
noIs the average word length above 5?
yesGerman?
noEnglish?

That flowchart is a decision tree (this one is only a sketch, and a rough one). In this page you watch a computer learn such a tree from examples: which questions to ask, in which order, and when to stop.

The same idea powers the language-identification exercise that follows this page. Here we use made-up measurements, so that we can see the whole picture at once.

1 · The data

Every example becomes a point

A computer cannot read a sentence the way you do. It needs measurements: numbers that describe each example. Here every example has two, feature 1 and feature 2, so it becomes one point on a plane. {{n}} examples, {{n}} points. (They are made up, so that we can see everything.)

1 · The data

The label is the answer

Each example also has a label: the answer we want the computer to learn. Two answers here: blue circle ({{nBlue}} examples) and orange diamond ({{nOrange}}). In your exercise the label will be a language.

1 · The data

A question cuts the plane in two

A tree asks yes/no questions about one measurement at a time. “Is feature 1 at most 5?” is a vertical line. Left of it: {{q5lb}} blue and {{q5lo}} orange. Right of it: {{q5rb}} blue and {{q5ro}} orange. A good question leaves each side more one-coloured than before.

1 · The data

Which question first?

Two candidates: a vertical cut (A) or a horizontal cut (B). Which separates the colours better?

The vertical cut, A. The best vertical cut scores {{gainV}}, the best horizontal one only {{gainH}}. The next chapters explain what that score is.
2 · A tree is a flowchart

Predicting is a walk down the tree

This is the tree the computer will learn in chapter 4, next to the plane it cuts. Each box is a node. A new example arrives (black ring). To label it we start at the top node, the root, and answer the questions on the way down.

2 · A tree is a flowchart

Question 1

The root asks: “is feature 1 at most {{rootThr}}?” Our example has feature 1 = {{probeX}}. Yes: go left. On the plane, everything left of the cut has taken the same first step.

2 · A tree is a flowchart

Question 2

Next: “is feature 2 at most {{leftThr}}?” Our example has {{probeY}}. No: go right. The region shrinks to one rectangle.

2 · A tree is a flowchart

A leaf gives the answer

A leaf has no more questions. {{leafO}} training examples ended up here, all orange, so the tree says orange. The answer is a majority vote inside the leaf: just counting.

3 · How mixed is a node?

Two picks from the bag

To choose good questions, the computer needs a ruler for “mixed up”. Put the {{n}} examples in a bag. Pick one, note its label, put it back, pick again. Do the two picks disagree? Press the button a few times: in the long run they disagree in about {{rootG}} of the tries. That number is the Gini impurity.

3 · How mixed is a node?

Compare three nodes

Which of these three bags is the most mixed up?

The left part: {{leftB}} blue, {{leftO}} orange, impurity {{leftG}}. The whole set scores {{rootG}}. The right part is all blue: 0, a pure node.
\(G \;=\; 1 - \Big[\big(\text{share of blue}\big)^2 + \big(\text{share of orange}\big)^2\Big]\)

Whole set: \(1-({{t:pB}}^2+{{t:pO}}^2)\approx {{t:rootG}}\)

3 · How mixed is a node?

From mixed to pure

Pure node: both picks always agree, 0. A fifty-fifty node is a coin flip, the messiest a two-label node can be: 0.5. Everything between is a smooth hill. A leaf is a node we stop at, ideally pure.

3 · How mixed is a node?

Another ruler: entropy optional

Information theory counts in bits: roughly, how many yes/no questions you still need to name the label. Whole set: {{rootH}} bits; coin flip: 1 bit; pure: 0. The two hills have the same shape, so they usually choose the same questions. We keep Gini. \(H = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k\)

4 · Finding the best question

Try every question

At each node the computer scores every possible question: each feature, each cut between neighbouring values. Drag the cut below the picture and watch both sides. The gain says how much cleaner the two sides are than the node before the cut.

\(\text{gain}=G(\text{before})-\Big[\tfrac{n_L}{n}\,G(\text{left})+\tfrac{n_R}{n}\,G(\text{right})\Big]\)
4 · Finding the best question

The best question wins

Of {{ncand}} cuts on feature 1, the highest gain, {{rootGain}}, is at {{rootThr}}. It becomes the root: the tree gains one node and the plane one cut, at the same moment. The right side is already pure: nothing left to ask there.

4 · Finding the best question

Then do it again on each side

The left side is still mixed ({{leftB}} blue, {{leftO}} orange). The computer repeats the same search on just those examples: best question, “feature 2 at most {{leftThr}}”. The same procedure, applied to a smaller group: that is the whole algorithm.

4 · Finding the best question

When does it stop?

A branch stops when it is pure, when it reaches the depth limit (here three questions in a row), or when too few examples are left. Both reasons show up here: pure leaves, and one mixed leaf ({{stopB}} blue, {{stopO}} orange) cut off by the depth limit.

5 · Decision boundaries

One question, two regions

Colour each region by the majority label of its examples: that is the tree's prediction there. After one question, {{acc1}} of the examples sit in a region of their own colour.

5 · Decision boundaries

Two questions, three rectangles

Each new question cuts one region again. With the second question {{acc2}} are right.

5 · Decision boundaries

Only straight, axis-parallel cuts

Every question looks at one feature, so every cut is parallel to an axis. A tree cannot draw a slanted line or a curve in one go; it can only imitate one with steps. That is the price of being easy to read.

5 · Decision boundaries

Depth is a dial

Drag the depth. From depth {{depthFull}} on, every region is pure, so the tree stops growing and nothing changes: {{leavesFull}} regions, {{acc4}} correct. Is “perfect on these examples” what we want? Next chapter.

6 · Overfitting

Real data is noisy

Now four examples (ringed) carry a label that contradicts their neighbourhood: a typing slip, a borderline case. A shallow tree (depth 2) simply ignores them and gets {{shTrain}} of the training examples right.

6 · Overfitting

A deep tree memorises

Depth 8 keeps asking questions until every training example is right: {{deepTrain}}. See the little islands around the ringed examples? The tree has learned accidents, not the pattern: {{leavesDeep}} regions instead of {{leavesShallow}}.

6 · Overfitting

Fresh examples decide

Now {{nFresh}} fresh examples from the same source (hollow shapes). Neither tree has seen them.

Will the deep tree still win?

No: {{deepFresh}} against {{shFresh}} for the shallow tree. The deep tree won on the examples it memorised and lost on new ones: that gap is overfitting.
6 · Overfitting

Find the sweet spot

Drag the depth. Accuracy on the training examples only ever goes up. Accuracy on fresh examples peaks at depth {{bestDepth}} ({{bestFresh}}) and then falls. That is why we always test on examples the model has never seen.

7 · Random forest

Resample the examples optional

One deep tree is jumpy: its islands depend on the exact examples. Idea: grow many trees, each on its own resample, drawn with replacement (“bootstrap”). Big shapes were drawn twice or more; dashed ones were left out: about {{oob}} of them. Press the button for another sample.

7 · Random forest

Many different trees optional

Each tree sees its own sample, and at each question it may look at only one randomly chosen feature (we have just two, so “a random subset” means one). Five trees, five different boundaries.

7 · Random forest

Let them vote optional

The forest labels a point by majority vote. Slide the number of trees: one tree is jagged, {{nTrees}} are smooth. On the fresh examples the forest is right {{forestFresh}} of the time; one deep tree {{deepFresh}}.

8 · Going further: boosting

Hard cases optional

Boosting also combines many small trees, but one after another, each fixing the last one's mistakes. One feature on a line: blue = −1, orange = +1. Two examples (ringed) sit on the wrong side. A stump is a tree with a single question.

8 · Going further: boosting

Round 1: the best stump optional

Start with equal weights. The best single question gets {{wrong1}} examples wrong. Its weighted share of mistakes is ε = {{eps1}}, and its say in the final vote is \(\alpha=\tfrac12\ln\dfrac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\approx {{t:alpha1}}\)

8 · Going further: boosting

Mistakes get heavier optional

Wrong examples grow, right ones shrink (circle size = weight). Afterwards the stump we just used is exactly as bad as a coin flip, so the next stump must find something new.

8 · Going further: boosting

{{rounds}} rounds, one vote optional

Repeat. Each stump votes with strength α; the sign of the total decides. After {{rounds}} rounds no training example is wrong. Slide through the rounds. (Forest = parallel trees; boosting = trees in a chain.)

9 · Recap

What the computer did

It looked at all questions of the form “is this measurement at most …?”, kept the one that left the two sides cleanest, and repeated on each side until it was pure or the depth limit was reached. Too deep and it memorises.

9 · Recap

Back to language

In the exercise an example is a text, the measurements are numbers computed from it (vowel ratio, average word length, how often letter clusters such as sch or th occur), and the label is the language. The tree you just watched is what scikit-learn will train for you.
