From a Yes to a Network

Where do neural networks come from, and what do they have to do with word vectors?

linear regression→ logistic regression→ a neuron→ a network→ word vectors

You already know the first two: a weighted sum, and the sigmoid that turns it into a yes/no probability. Stack that machine on itself and you have a neural network. Point it at running text and its inner weights turn into the word vectors that word2vec is famous for.

The data on this page is made up (blobs of points, a 14-word corpus), except the movie-review example from class. Everything is trained live in your browser. If linear and logistic regression feel rusty, visit From a Number to a Yes first.

1 · One yes/no machine

Weighted evidence, then a squish

You know this machine: weight each feature, then add up. Here a movie review is six hand-made numbers (the example from class). Each weight times its feature is evidence for (blue) or against (orange); the total is the score z.

Logistic regression adds the sigmoid, which turns any score into a probability. This review: z = {{z}}, so {{pPct}}% positive. Move the sliders.

\(z=b+w_1x_1+\dots+w_6x_6,\qquad P(\text{positive})=\sigma(z)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\)

The last feature is ln of the word count: length, tamed by a log.

1 · One yes/no machine

How wrong is a probability?

Training needs a penalty. Take the probability the model gave to the right answer. Sure and right costs almost nothing; sure and wrong costs the most. Training lowers the average penalty, the cross-entropy loss.

\(\text{penalty}=-\ln p_{\text{right}}\)

Flip the true label under the figure. This is the log-loss of From a Number to a Yes.

1 · One yes/no machine

When the data can be split perfectly optional

Imagine twelve invented reviews that one threshold separates perfectly. A steeper sigmoid is then always a little better, so without a penalty the weight never stops growing and the loss only creeps towards 0: after {{sepSteps}} steps it has reached {{sepW0}}.

You met the cure in ElasticNet: punish large weights. Scroll to train, then pick a penalty size. (Stopping early works too.)

\(\text{loss}=\text{cross-entropy}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,w^2\)

L1 and ElasticNet are explained in Same Units, Fair Distances.

1 · One yes/no machine

More than two answers: softmax

Positive, neutral or negative? Give each class its own score; softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 1. It is the sigmoid for many classes. The right answer is written as a one-hot vector, and the penalty is the same −ln of its probability.

\(P(k)=\dfrac{e^{z_k}}{e^{z_1}+e^{z_2}+e^{z_3}}\)

Remember softmax: at the end of this page the “classes” are the words of a language.

Activation
2 · A neuron

Logistic regression, drawn as a circle

Draw the review model as a diagram: six inputs, six weighted edges (thicker means a bigger weight; blue pushes up, orange pushes down) and one circle that adds them up and applies an activation function. That circle is a neuron.

Only the squish is a free choice. Sigmoid gives a probability. ReLU, max(0, z), just cuts off negatives and is popular inside networks. Switch below.

2 · A neuron

Brain or bending? optional

The name comes from biology. 1943: McCulloch and Pitts model a neuron as a threshold switch. 1958: Rosenblatt's perceptron learns its weights from examples (with a step, not a sigmoid).

But real neurons spike at varying frequencies, and brains seem to learn differently. Better to think of a network as a chain of learned bendings of data, not a model of the brain.

3 · One neuron is not enough

Add a hidden layer

Put a layer of neurons between the inputs and the answer. Each hidden neuron draws its own line (dashed); the output neuron combines their answers into curved regions. Try 2, 3 and 8: accuracy {{acc2}}%, {{acc3}}% and {{acc8}}%.

Same sigmoid neurons as before: a network is neurons feeding neurons.

3 · One neuron is not enough

Bend the space, then draw a line

What does the output neuron actually see? Not the original points but the two hidden neurons' answers. Scroll and watch the points move into that new space: there, one straight line separates them.

This is the kernel trick of the SVM page, except that the bending is learned from data. A network is a chain of learned, non-linear projections.

4 · Layers and the forward pass

Layers, and who chooses their size

The inputs form the input layer, the last neurons the output layer, everything between the hidden layers. The data fixes two sizes: inputs = number of features (6 for our reviews), outputs = number of classes (3). The hidden size is your choice.

Predict: a network turns one English word into one German word. How big are its input and output layers?

One per word in each vocabulary: a one-hot input for the English word, a softmax output over the German words (we meet this again in chapter 7). Sentences vary in length, which is why real translation needs fancier networks.
4 · Layers and the forward pass

Using a network: the forward pass

To use a trained network, feed numbers in and let them flow: multiply, add, squish, layer after layer. Move the two inputs. Nothing is learned here; the weights stay fixed. This is the forward pass.

This small network is the one trained on the blobs of chapter 3 (two hidden neurons).

5 · Learning

Who is to blame?

Training starts with a guess: run one example forward and measure the penalty. Then the blame flows backwards: every weight gets a number for how much it contributed to the error, and gradient descent nudges it the other way. This is backpropagation. Scroll through the four phases.

“Blame” is the gradient of the loss for that weight, found layer by layer with the chain rule. It happens only during training.

5 · Learning

Repeat, many times

One nudge is tiny. Training repeats it over all the examples, again and again; one pass over all the data is an epoch. Scroll to watch {{epochs}} epochs: the loss falls and the regions bend around the blobs.

5 · Learning

What training does not learn

Training learns only the weights and biases. You choose everything else, the hyper-parameters, and the loss must fit the task: squared error for numbers, cross-entropy for classes.

Frameworks (PyTorch, Keras, HuggingFace, spaCy) do most of the work. Start from an existing model and change what you need.

6 · Words as numbers

Every word equally unrelated

A network needs numbers, so give each word a one-hot vector: a 1 in its own position, zeros elsewhere. With 100,000 words, that is 100,000 numbers per word.

And the cosine similarity of any two different one-hot words is 0: cheese is as unrelated to bread as to sheep.

Cosine similarity: 1 means two vectors point the same way, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite.

6 · Words as numbers

The company a word keeps

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” Count which words occur near which (the PMI page does this carefully). A word's row of counts describes its company.

Compare rows with cosine similarity: cheese and bread, whose rows look alike, score {{cc 0 1}}; bread and sheep score {{cc 1 3}}. Pick a pair below.

6 · Words as numbers

Two routes to short, dense vectors

Rows of counts are long and mostly zeros. We want a few hundred numbers per word. Route 1: count, then compress the table (SVD, as in class). Route 2: train a model to predict words from their neighbours, the next word or a missing one (a cloze test), and keep what it learns.

The next two chapters take route 2.

7 · From counting to a network

A Markov chain: what follows what

A tiny made-up corpus: {{nSent}} sentences, {{pairs}} pairs of neighbouring words. Draw each word as a node and link A to B with the probability that B follows A. That graph is a Markov chain. Pick a word to see its outgoing probabilities.

Predict: how likely is pool right after motel?

Exactly zero: “motel pool” never occurs in the corpus. A Markov chain is a table of counts: it knows only the pairs it has seen, and every word stands alone.
7 · From counting to a network

An inner matrix, one row per word

Now a neural network. Its input is a one-hot vector. Multiply it by an inner matrix with one row per word and the 1 picks out that word's row: just two numbers.

This embedding layer is a lookup table too, but its rows are a few learned numbers, not counts. They start random and are changed by backpropagation like any other weights.

7 · From counting to a network

Predict the next word

Behind the inner matrix sit more weights and a softmax over all {{V}} words: the probability of each next word. Labels come free: in running text, the next word is the label. Bars: the network. Ticks: the Markov table.

With no activation in the hidden layer, every word squeezes through the same two numbers, so words with similar followers end up with similar rows.

The neural language model of Bengio et al. (2003), trained for {{embEpochs}} epochs.

8 · Similar words, nearby rows

Similar words, similar rows

Scroll to train. Each word's two numbers are an arrow from the centre. Nobody said hotel and motel are alike, but they keep the same company, so their arrows turn the same way: cosine similarity {{cosHM}}. Goat and sheep: {{cosGS}}.

The unseen pair: after motel the network gives pool {{pMotelPool}}, after goat {{pGoatPool}}. The Markov chain says 0 for both.

Real embeddings have 50 to 1000 numbers per word, not 2.

8 · Similar words, nearby rows

Where word2vec comes in

This surprise started the search for better word vectors, and word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) was one of the first. As you read about it, look for how it differs from our toy:

  • neighbours on both sides: skip-gram and CBOW
  • negative sampling instead of a softmax over every word
  • how vectors are judged: cosine neighbours, analogy tests
  • what came next: FastText, BPE, contextual vectors (ELMo, BERT, GPT)
