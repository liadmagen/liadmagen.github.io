The Widest Street

Draw the widest street between two groups, and when no street fits, lift the data.

Many straight lines split two groups. Which one is best?→the widest street (the margin) What if no straight line can split them at all?→lift the data (the kernel trick) support vector machines in nine short chapters

You will see why only a few dots matter, how a softer street copes with messy data, and how a cluster inside a ring becomes separable once it is lifted into a third dimension. At the end, the scikit-learn code.

All data on this page is made up: {{nSep}}, {{nOvl}}, {{nRing}} and {{nMoon}} invented dots in four small sets, so that every dot is visible, plus {{nFresh}} fresh dots for each set. The ideas are the ones behind many text classifiers.

1 · Many lines

Many lines, all correct

Each dot is a made-up document described by two numbers. Group A (circles) and group B (diamonds) can be split by a straight line, and not by just one: these five lines each get all {{nSep}} dots right. A model that learns from examples has to choose.

1 · Many lines

Which one would you trust?

New dots will arrive, some close to the gap between the groups. The shaded strip around each line is its empty “street”.

Predict: which line will make the fewest mistakes on fresh dots?

Line {{widestLetter}}. Its street is the widest, {{sepMargin}}. On {{nFresh}} fresh dots it is right {{svmFresh}}; the others {{candMin}} to {{candMax}}.
1 · Many lines

The widest street

A support vector machine (SVM) picks the line with the widest empty street around it, the margin. Tilt the line: drag the figure or use the slider. The curve shows the street width at every tilt. It peaks at {{sepMargin}}, and some tilts cannot separate the groups at all.

\(\text{street width}=\dfrac{2}{\lVert w\rVert}\)

Here w is an arrow across the street: the longer it is, the narrower the street.

2 · Support vectors

Only a few dots hold the street

The dots that touch the edges of the street are the support vectors (ringed). Here {{sepNSV}} of {{nSep}} dots are support vectors. Every other dot could sit anywhere further out, and the street would not change.

2 · Support vectors

Remove a far-away dot

Scroll to take away the dot farthest from the street. Nothing happens: the street is {{sepMargin}} wide before and {{farMargin}} after. The same holds for each of the other {{nFar}} dots that are not support vectors.

2 · Support vectors

Move a support vector

Now slide one support vector {{moveBy}} units toward the street. The street has to follow: it narrows from {{sepMargin}} to {{movedMargin}} and turns a little. The dashed grey street is the old one.

2 · Support vectors

Your turn

Pick a dot, move it, remove it, and watch the street refit. Far-away dots do nothing; support vectors change everything. scikit-learn keeps exactly these dots in support_vectors_.

Keyboard: Tab to the figure, then use the arrow keys to move the selected dot.

3 · Soft margin

When the groups overlap

Real data is messy. Here the two groups overlap a little, so no straight street can be clean. A strict SVM (C = {{hardC}}) squeezes in a street only {{ovlHardMargin}} wide and still gets {{ovlHardErr}} dot wrong.

3 · Soft margin

C: how strict is the street?

C is the price of a mistake. A large C punishes every dot on the wrong side, so the street gets narrow. A small C forgives some mistakes and keeps the street wide. From strict (C = {{cBigC}}) to relaxed (C = {{cSmallC}}), the street grows from {{cBigMargin}} to {{cSmallMargin}} and the support vectors from {{cBigNSV}} to {{cSmallNSV}}.

3 · Soft margin

C is a regularization knob

Compare training accuracy with accuracy on {{nFresh}} fresh dots. Both extremes do worse; C = 1, the scikit-learn default, gets {{c1Fresh}} on fresh dots. In scikit-learn a smaller C means stronger regularization, like the λ on the scaling and generalization page, where a bigger λ meant a stronger penalty.

4 · No line fits

A cluster inside a ring

Now group A is a small cluster and group B is a ring around it: {{nRing}} made-up dots. Imagine one straight line with all of A on one side and all of B on the other. There is none, because the ring surrounds the cluster.

4 · No line fits

A straight street fails

The best straight street an SVM can find gets {{linTrain}} of these dots right, and {{linFresh}} of {{nFresh}} fresh ones. That is barely better than guessing. The red rings mark the mistakes.

4 · No line fits

Try every line

Turn a line through the picture: drag the figure or use the slider. At each angle it sits where it gets the most dots right.

Predict: can any straight line get 90% right?

No. Over all 360 directions and every position, the best line gets {{bestLine}}. The data is fine; a straight line is the wrong tool.
5 · Lift it

Add a third number

Give every dot a height: its squared distance from the centre. Scroll to lift them. The cluster stays near the floor, the ring rises high.

\(z=x_1^2+x_2^2\)
5 · Lift it

A flat sheet now fits

In 3-D the groups sit at different heights: the cluster reaches at most {{zMaxA}}, the ring starts at {{zMinB}}. A flat sheet at height {{liftC}} separates them with {{liftGap}} to spare. A flat sheet is a straight line's bigger sibling.

5 · Lift it

Drop it back down

Where the sheet cuts the bowl, the dots' distance from the centre is {{liftRadius}}: a circle. Let that circle fall to the floor and look from above. Back in 2-D, it separates cluster from ring.

5 · Lift it

Turn it around

Drag the picture to rotate it, or use the buttons or arrow keys. Slide the circle back up onto the sheet. Nothing was curved in 3-D: the curve in 2-D is a flat cut seen from above.

6 · The kernel trick

The SVM only asks: how similar?

Inside, an SVM never needs a dot's position, only how similar two dots are. A kernel is a similarity score. Pick a reference dot (the cross) and a kernel: the colour shows how similar every place is to it.

6 · The kernel trick

Lifting without lifting

The degree-2 polynomial kernel scores two points as the square of their dot product. Lifting both into 3-D and taking the dot product there gives the very same number. The kernel stays in 2-D and never builds the lifted points.

\((x\cdot y)^2=\varphi(x)\cdot\varphi(y)\) \(\varphi(x)=\left(x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2\right)\)
6 · The kernel trick

A kernel that fades with distance

The RBF kernel scores similarity as a bump that fades with distance; γ sets how fast. It matches a lift into infinitely many new numbers, which no computer could build, yet the kernel is one short formula.

\(k(x,y)=e^{-\gamma\,\lVert x-y\rVert^2}\)
7 · Kernels side by side

Four kernels, two shapes

Same dots, different kernels. A line fails on both shapes. The polynomial of degree 2 gets {{ringP2}} of fresh ring dots, but the moons need more bend: degree 3 gets {{moonP3}} of fresh moon dots, against {{moonLin}} for a line. Switch shapes and kernels under the figure.

7 · Kernels side by side

γ: how far a dot's voice carries

For the RBF kernel, a small γ gives wide, smooth bumps; a large γ gives narrow ones, so the boundary forms little islands around single dots. Slide γ to the far right: {{gBigSV}} of {{nMoon}} moon dots become support vectors.

7 · Kernels side by side

Islands do not generalize

Training accuracy stays near 100%, but fresh accuracy falls from {{gBestFresh}} at a moderate γ to {{gBigFresh}} at the largest. That is overfitting, as on the scaling and generalization page. A tiny γ underfits instead: {{gTinyTrain}} even on training dots.

1 · Fit and look inside
import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import SVC

# Twenty made-up points, two numbers each: the dots of chapters 1 and 2.
X = np.array([
    [1.79, 0.93], [-1.34, -0.64], [0.77, 1.13], [-1.16, -0.73],
    [1.81, 0.33], [-1.00, -0.71], [0.77, 1.60], [-2.70, -1.26],
    [1.73, 1.35], [-1.98, -2.18], [1.46, 0.92], [-1.71, -1.61],
    [1.93, 1.92], [-1.69, -1.49], [1.40, 0.81], [-1.31, -1.31],
    [1.30, -0.20], [-1.19, -0.47], [1.53, 2.25], [-0.79, -0.88],
])
y = [1, -1] * 10                          # 1 = group A, -1 = group B

clf = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1000)        # a huge C: (almost) no mistakes allowed
clf.fit(X, y)

print(clf.support_vectors_)               # the points on the edge of the street
print(clf.support_)                       # which rows of X they are
print(clf.n_support_)                     # how many per group (-1 first, then 1)
print(2 / np.linalg.norm(clf.coef_))      # street width = 2 / length of w
print(clf.predict([[2.0, 1.0], [-2.0, -1.0]]))

2 · Scale, then fit
from sklearn.datasets import make_circles
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

# A cluster inside a ring, like chapter 4.
X, y = make_circles(n_samples=200, noise=0.1, factor=0.3, random_state=0)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)

# Scale first, then the SVM. The pipeline remembers the scaling numbers
# from the training data and reuses them on new data.
rbf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1, gamma="scale"))
rbf.fit(X_train, y_train)

line = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="linear"))
line.fit(X_train, y_train)

print("rbf    ", rbf.score(X_test, y_test))
print("linear ", line.score(X_test, y_test))
print("support vectors:", rbf[-1].n_support_.sum(), "of", len(X_train))
Output
rbf     1.0
linear  0.56
support vectors: 23 of 150
3 · Tune C and gamma
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=200, noise=0.25, random_state=0)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf"))
grid = {"svc__C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100],          # step name, two underscores, parameter
        "svc__gamma": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}

# Try every pair with 5-fold cross-validation on the training data only.
search = GridSearchCV(pipe, grid, cv=5)
search.fit(X_train, y_train)

print(search.best_params_)
print("cross-validated:", round(search.best_score_, 3))
print("fresh test data:", round(search.score(X_test, y_test), 3))
Output
{'svc__C': 10, 'svc__gamma': 1}
cross-validated: 0.913
fresh test data: 0.96
4 · Language identification
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.svm import LinearSVC

train = [
    ("The weather is lovely today, so we are going for a walk.", "en"),
    ("She has been learning to translate legal contracts for two years.", "en"),
    ("Could you please send me the final version of the document?", "en"),
    ("My brother lives in a small town near the sea.", "en"),
    ("Das Wetter ist heute schön, deshalb gehen wir spazieren.", "de"),
    ("Sie lernt seit zwei Jahren, juristische Verträge zu übersetzen.", "de"),
    ("Könntest du mir bitte die endgültige Fassung des Dokuments schicken?", "de"),
    ("Mein Bruder wohnt in einer kleinen Stadt am Meer.", "de"),
    ("Hoy hace muy buen tiempo, así que vamos a dar un paseo.", "es"),
    ("Ella lleva dos años aprendiendo a traducir contratos legales.", "es"),
    ("¿Podrías enviarme la versión final del documento, por favor?", "es"),
    ("Mi hermano vive en un pueblo pequeño cerca del mar.", "es"),
]
texts, labels = zip(*train)

# Count short letter sequences (1 to 3 letters), weight them with TF-IDF,
# then draw straight-line streets between the three languages.
model = make_pipeline(
    TfidfVectorizer(analyzer="char_wb", ngram_range=(1, 3)),
    LinearSVC(),
)
model.fit(texts, labels)

new = [
    "We will translate the manual before the end of the month.",
    "Wir übersetzen das Handbuch noch vor Ende des Monats.",
    "Vamos a traducir el manual antes de que acabe el mes.",
]
print(model.classes_)                     # the order of the columns below
print(model.predict(new))                 # one language code per sentence
print(model.decision_function(new).round(2))   # one score per language

5 · The kernel trick by hand (optional)
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_circles
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_circles(n_samples=100, noise=0.1, factor=0.3, random_state=0)

def gram(A, B):
    """Similarity of every row of A with every row of B: (a . b) squared."""
    return (A @ B.T) ** 2

# Hand the SVM the table of similarities instead of the points.
by_hand = SVC(kernel="precomputed").fit(gram(X, X), y)
print(by_hand.score(gram(X, X), y))

# scikit-learn can do the same thing by itself: (gamma * a . b + coef0) ** degree
built_in = SVC(kernel="poly", degree=2, gamma=1, coef0=0).fit(X, y)
print(built_in.score(X, y))
print(np.array_equal(by_hand.support_, built_in.support_))

True

8 · scikit-learn

Fit, then look inside

SVC is scikit-learn's SVM. Fit it on the dots of chapters 1 and 2, then read off the support vectors, their row numbers and counts, and the street width from coef_. A huge C approximates a hard margin.

8 · scikit-learn

Scale, then fit

An SVM measures distances, so scale features first. A Pipeline keeps scaler and model together and reuses the training numbers on new data. Swap kernel between "linear", "poly" and "rbf"; gamma="scale" is the default.

8 · scikit-learn

Tune C and γ

GridSearchCV tries every pair of values with cross-validation inside the training data, so the test data stays untouched until the very end. Never choose C and γ by looking at the test set.

8 · scikit-learn

Text: TF-IDF and LinearSVC

For text, turn each sentence into TF-IDF numbers and use LinearSVC, a fast SVM with a straight street. Here it tells English, German and Spanish apart. It gives scores, not probabilities: decision_function. probability=True on SVC adds Platt scaling, but is slower.

9 · Recap

The big idea in four lines

Margin, soft margin, kernel, γ: each answers one question about drawing a boundary between two groups.

9 · Recap

When SVMs shine, and when not

They shine with many features and moderate numbers of examples, which is why TF-IDF with a linear SVM was a strong text baseline for years. They struggle when the data is huge.

9 · Recap

Rules of thumb

Scale the features, start with a linear street for text, try RBF for small dense data, choose C and γ by cross-validation, and judge the result on fresh data.
